Queen

The queen (♕, ♛) is the most powerful piece in the game of chess. It can move any number of squares vertically, horizontally or diagonally, combining the powers of the rook and bishop. Each player starts the game with one queen, placed in the middle of the first rank next to the king. Because the queen is the strongest piece, a pawn is promoted to a queen in the vast majority of cases; if a pawn is promoted to a piece other than a queen, it is an underpromotion. Because of its ability to control many squares at once, the queen plays a major role in both offensive and defensive strategies. It can launch powerful attacks, protect important pieces, and create threats that force an opponent to respond. Although the queen is the strongest piece, skilled players usually avoid bringing it into the game too early because it can easily become the target of attacks from less valuable pieces. In terms of relative value, the queen is worth about nine points, making it the most valuable piece on the board after the king. Learning how to use the queen wisely is an important part of becoming a better chess player, as it can greatly influence the outcome of a game when used effectively.

King

The king ♔, ♚) is the most important piece in the game of chess. It may move to any adjacent square that is not controlled by an enemy piece; it may also perform, in tandem with the rook, a move called castling. If a player's king is threatened with capture, it is said to be in check, and the player must remove or evade the threat of capture immediately, such as by moving it away from the attacked square. If this cannot be done, the king is said to be in checkmate, resulting in a loss for the checkmated player. A player cannot make any move that places their own king in check. Despite this, the king can become a strong offensive piece in the endgame or, rarely, the middlegame. The king can move one square in any direction—forward, backward, sideways, or diagonally—as long as the destination square is not under attack by an opponent's piece. Unlike the knight, the king cannot jump over other pieces and cannot move onto a square occupied by one of its own pieces. It captures enemy pieces by moving one square to the piece's square, provided that square is safe. Another special move involving the king is castling, which is the only move in chess where two pieces move at the same time. During castling, the king moves two squares toward a rook, and the rook moves to the square next to the king. Castling is only allowed if neither the king nor the rook has moved before, there are no pieces between them, the king is not currently in check, and the king does not move through or end on a square that is under attack.

Rook

The rook (/rʊk/; ♖, ♜) is a piece in the game of chess. It may move any number of squares horizontally or vertically without jumping, and it may capture an enemy piece on its path; it may participate in castling. Each player starts the game with two rooks, one in each corner on their side of the board. The rook is a powerful piece because it can control entire rows and columns of the board. It is especially effective on open files and ranks where there are few or no pieces blocking its path. In the opening stage of the game, rooks are usually less active because they are often blocked by pawns and other pieces. As the game progresses and the board opens up, they become much more powerful and are commonly used to attack enemy pieces, defend important positions, and support the promotion of pawns. Two rooks working together can be extremely strong, as they can control multiple files and ranks at the same time.

Bishop

he bishop (♗, ♝) is a piece in the game of chess. It moves and captures along diagonals without jumping over interfering pieces. Each player begins the game with two bishops. The starting squares are c1 and f1 for White's bishops, and c8 and f8 for Black's bishops. One unique characteristic of the bishop is that it always stays on the same color of squares throughout the game. A bishop that starts on a light-colored square can only move on light-colored squares, while a bishop that starts on a dark-colored square can only move on dark-colored squares. Because of this, the two bishops work best when used together, as they can control both light and dark squares across the board. Bishops are especially powerful on open boards where there are fewer pawns blocking their movement, allowing them to attack from long distances and support other pieces. The bishop is an important piece in both attacking and defending. It can protect key squares, pin enemy pieces, and create powerful combinations with other pieces, especially the queen and rook. During the opening, bishops are often developed early to control the center of the board and prepare for castling. In the middle and endgame, bishops become even more valuable because their long-range movement allows them to influence large areas of the board. Learning how to use bishops effectively and coordinating the pair of bishops can provide a significant strategic advantage and greatly improve a player's chances of winning.

Knight

The knight (♘, ♞) is a piece in the game of chess. It moves two squares vertically and one square horizontally, or two squares horizontally and one square vertically, jumping over other pieces. Each player starts the game with two knights on the b- and g-files, each located between a rook and a bishop. The knight captures an opponent's piece by moving to the square where that piece is located. Because of its unusual movement, the knight can attack pieces that other chess pieces may not be able to reach easily. A knight can control up to eight squares at once when placed near the center of the board, which makes central positions very important for knights. However, knights are less effective when placed near the edges or corners of the board because they have fewer possible moves. Knights are especially useful during the opening and middle stages of the game. they can help control the center, defend important pieces, create attacks, and set up tactical opportunities such as forks, where a knight attacks two or more enemy pieces at the same time. In the endgame, knights can still be powerful, especially when the board is more open, although they may struggle against fast-moving pieces like rooks and bishops. Learning how to position and use knights effectively is an important part of chess strategy because their unique movement can create unexpected threats and advantages against an opponent.

Pawn

The pawn (♙, ♟) is the most numerous and weakest piece in the game of chess. It can move one vacant square directly forward, or one or two vacant squares directly forward on its first move, and can capture one square diagonally forward. Each player begins a game with eight pawns, one on each square of their second rank. The white pawns start on a2 through h2, while the black pawns start on a7 through h7. Pawns are often called the "foundation" of a chess position because they help control the center of the board, protect other pieces, and create defensive structures. Although each pawn is only worth about one point, they can become very powerful when used correctly. Pawns can block enemy attacks, support other pieces, and create opportunities for strategic plays. The position of pawns often determines the overall strength of a player's plan because moving them can create weaknesses or open important lines for rooks, bishops, and queens. One of the most important abilities of a pawn is promotion. When a pawn reaches the opposite end of the chessboard, it can be promoted into another piece, usually a queen, but it can also become a rook, bishop, or knight. This allows a pawn to transform into a much stronger piece and can completely change the outcome of a game. Pawns also have special rules, such as en passant, a rare capture move that can happen when an opponent's pawn moves two squares forward from its starting position and lands beside your pawn. Understanding how to use pawns effectively is essential in chess because even the smallest pieces can have a major impact on the result of the game.
